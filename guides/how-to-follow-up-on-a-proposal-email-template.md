# How to Follow Up on a Proposal (Without Being Annoying)

You sent the proposal. Three days of silence. A week. Most freelancers either panic-follow-up ("Just bumping this to the top of your inbox!!") or give up entirely. Both lose the job.

Here's the system that works.

## Why proposals go quiet

Almost never because the answer is no. The real reasons, in order:

1. **The decision isn't theirs alone.** Your contact is selling it internally, and that meeting hasn't happened yet.
2. **Priority shift.** Something urgent ate their week. Your proposal is in the "important, not urgent" pile.
3. **Sticker shock they're embarrassed to mention.** They want to negotiate but don't know how to start.
4. **They genuinely forgot.** Inbox zero is a myth.

Notice what's not on the list: you. Silence is about their process, not your proposal.

## The cadence

| Touch | Timing | Goal |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3 business days after sending | Light nudge + add value |
| 2 | 7 days after touch 1 | Surface the objection |
| 3 | 7 days after touch 2 | The takeaway (close the file) |

Then stop. Three touches is professional persistence; six is spam.

## Touch 1 — the nudge (day 3)

> Subject: Quick question about the [project] proposal
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> Sent over the proposal on [day] — wanted to make sure it didn't get buried.
>
> One thing I've been thinking about since: [one specific idea or observation about their project — a quick win, a risk to watch, a relevant example]. Happy to walk you through it either way.
>
> Is the timeline on your end still [their stated timeline]?
>
> [Your name]

Why it works: you gave them a reason to reply that isn't "please decide," and the closing question is easy to answer.

## Touch 2 — surface the objection (day 10)

> Subject: Re: [project] proposal — totally fine if the answer's no
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> Following up once more on the [project] proposal. If it's a fit issue or the budget landed wrong, genuinely no problem — I'd rather know than guess.
>
> If it's just timing, tell me when to check back and I'll diarize it.
>
> [Your name]

Giving explicit permission to say no is the single highest-response-rate move in freelance follow-up. It removes the social cost of replying.

## Touch 3 — the takeaway (day 17)

> Subject: Closing the loop on [project]
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> I'll assume priorities shifted and close this out on my end. If [project] comes back around next quarter, I'd still be glad to do it — just reply here and we'll pick it up.
>
> Either way, thanks for considering me.
>
> [Your name]

A surprising share of "dead" proposals revive at exactly this email. Scarcity plus a graceful exit.

## Rules

- Never follow up the same week you sent it.
- Every touch adds something (an idea, an out, a deadline) — none are pure "checking in."
- Keep each email under 100 words.
- After touch 3, move the lead to a quarterly check-in list and get back to finding new clients.
