# How to Raise Your Freelance Rates: The Email Script + Timing Formula

*Most freelancers wait years too long to raise their rates — then lose the nerve at the moment of sending. Here's the formula and the exact email.*

## When to raise your rates: the signals

Raise when any two of these are true:

1. **You're fully booked** — turning away work or maintaining a waitlist. Excess demand is the market telling you your price is too low.
2. **You haven't raised in 12+ months.** Your skills, speed, and results have grown; your price should follow.
3. **New clients say yes instantly.** Zero price resistance usually means you underpriced.
4. **You resent the work at current rates.** Resentment is a pricing signal, not a character flaw.

## The timing formula

Every successful rate-increase message contains three elements:

1. **Notice** — 6 to 8 weeks before the change takes effect. Respect creates acceptance; surprises create churn.
2. **A reason** — demand, results, or investment in your craft. It doesn't need to be dramatic; it needs to be true.
3. **A deadline** — a cutoff date before which work booked at the old rate is honored. This is generous to the client *and* creates a legitimate reason for fence-sitters to book now.

Send it individually to each ongoing client. Not a mass announcement, not buried in an invoice footer.

## The rate-increase email (copy and personalize)

```
Subject: A heads-up on my rates for [year/period]

Hi [First name],

I wanted to let you know personally: starting [date, 6–8 weeks
out], my rate for [service] moves from [$X] to [$Y].

A few reasons: [demand for my time has grown / I've invested in
[skill/tool], and the results — like [specific result for this
client] — reflect that].

Anything we kick off before [date] is billed at the current
rate, so if you've been thinking about [project idea], now's a
good moment.

You've been one of my favorite clients to work with, and I'd
love to keep that going. Happy to jump on a call if you'd like
to talk through what this means for [their ongoing work].

[Your name]
```

## The three responses you'll get

**Acceptance (most common).** Most clients simply accept — especially good clients, who already suspected you were underpriced. Reply warmly and move on. Done.

**Negotiation.** Decide your walk-away number *before* sending. Options: hold firm ("I understand — unfortunately the new rate is what allows me to keep delivering at this level"), offer a middle step (smaller increase now, full increase in 6 months), or reduce scope to fit their budget (same rate, less work — this keeps your effective rate intact).

**Departure.** Some clients will leave. Here's the math nobody tells you: losing your bottom 10% of clients by price usually *raises* your total income, because the freed capacity sells at the new rate. The clients who leave over a 15% increase were your most price-sensitive — and usually your most demanding — clients.

## New clients vs. existing clients

Quote the new rate to every new prospect immediately — no notice period needed. The increase applies only to your existing book. Many freelancers discover that the "scary" new rate is simply accepted by strangers, which retroactively proves the increase was overdue.

## After the increase

- Update your rate everywhere: proposals, packages, website, marketplace profiles.
- Put the next review date in your calendar, 12 months out. Annual small increases beat occasional large jumps.
- Track whether close rates change. If close rates don't drop at all, you're still underpriced.
